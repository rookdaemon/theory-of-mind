About the book
In "The Lifecycle of Software Objects," Ted Chiang explores
the intricate journey of artificial intelligence creation through
the lens of two individuals and the sentient beings they
nurture. Drawing inspiration from Alan Turing's philosophical
musings, Chiang imagines a contemporary landscape where
startup culture, multiplayer gaming, and open-source software
intersect. This novella, his longest to date at over 30,000
words, delves into more than a decade of evolution for both
the AIs and their creators as they navigate the challenges of
technological advancement, obsolescence, and the essence of
genuine connection with artificial entities. Through this
narrative, Chiang raises profound questions about the nature of
intelligence versus mere processing power and what it truly
means to form meaningful relationships in a rapidly evolving
digital age.

About the author
Ted Chiang is an acclaimed American speculative fiction
writer known for his thought-provoking narratives. Born as
Chiang Feng-nan, he graduated from Brown University with a
degree in Computer Science and currently works as a technical
writer in the software industry, residing in Bellevue,
Washington. A graduate of the prestigious Clarion Writers
Workshop in 1989, Chiang has garnered numerous prestigious
awards for his works, including multiple Nebula, Hugo, and
Locus Awards. His notable stories, such as "Tower of
Babylon" and "Story of Your Life," have solidified his
reputation, despite his limited output of only eleven short
stories as of 2009. Chiang's unique blend of technical insight
and imaginative storytelling has made him one of the most
celebrated voices in contemporary science fiction.

Summary of Chapter 2: The Lifecycle of Software
Objects
In Chapter 2 of "The Lifecycle of Software Objects," Blue
Gamma's digients have become an unexpected success, with
a vibrant community forming around their virtual pets.
Utilizing a "razor and blades" business model, Blue Gamma
generates income by selling digient food rather than the
digients themselves, encouraging customers to keep their
digients entertained for hours on end. This leads to the birth
of playgrounds, daycare centers, training classes, and events
dedicated to these digital companions.
Digients, each uniquely developing their personalities despite
sharing the same genomic foundation, showcase the
unpredictable nature of their growth, much to the joy and
challenge of their owners. Customers engage in lively
discussions on online forums, consulting one another for

advice on managing their digients’ behaviors. Derek, one of
Blue Gamma’s employees, enjoys exploring these forums,
sharing insights to help owners understand the complexity of
raising sentient virtual beings, contrasting their conscious
experiences with the mindless nature of typical video games.
The chapter also introduces the competitive landscape, as
other companies begin to offer their own genomic engines
for language learning, though none match the popularity of
Blue Gamma’s Neuroblast digients. Amidst this competition,
Blue Gamma receives a new prototype—a humanoid robot
body designed to bring digients into the real world, providing
a tangible connection for owners. This innovation excites
Ana, who sees potential in experiencing physical interactions
with her digient, Jax.
As they test this robot body, the digients confront the novelty
and curiosity inherent in the physical world, captivated by
textures they’ve never felt before. Marco and Polo, the
company’s mascots, express their delight and confusion
about the realities of the outside world, fostering a
connection between digital and physical experiences. Derek's
work on enhancing digient facial expressions is validated as
Marco exhibits a range of emotions during their outing.
Meanwhile, Derek grapples with new assignments that lack
the excitement of working directly with digients, as he is

tasked with creating avatars for a new breed of artificial life
called Xenotherians. This project poses challenges, and he
wonders if the intellectual rewards of such work will
compensate for the emotional connections he's grown to
cherish through his interactions with Marco and Polo.

Summary of Chapter 3 of "The Lifecycle of
Software Objects"
In the third chapter, the outlook for Blue Gamma, the
company developing digital pets known as digients, shifts
from hopeful to grim. Sales stagnate as existing customers
begin suspending their digients, finding them demanding as
they mature. Blue Gamma had aimed for digients that were
smart and obedient, but their evolution has made them
increasingly challenging, like a frustrating game. This leaves
many owners feeling unprepared, leading to the
establishment of volunteer rescue shelters for unwanted
digients, although these efforts struggle to find new homes
for them.
Ana, one of the key trainers of the digients, witnesses this
troubling trend but tries to remain optimistic. During a trip to
a playground, a reunion with Tibo, an older digient, offers a
moment of joy amid the chaos. It's a bittersweet interaction
as the digients cope with the notion of time and absence,
sparking deeper conversations about their existence and fears
of being suspended—which Jax perceptively expresses as a

concern about missing out on experiences. Ana reassures him
but grapples with her own worries about the future of these
digital beings.
As time progresses, Blue Gamma officially shuts down,
unable to retain enough customers willing to embrace the less
demanding digients they developed as a solution. Ana
reflects on her past experiences with loss, particularly with a
zoo closure, feeling a familiar ache despite knowing the
mascots won’t suffer like living animals would. With
employees hesitant to adopt the digients, Ana finds herself
contemplating what adoption means in the wake of loss and
the implications of care—echoed in a conversation with her
colleague Robyn, who shares her pregnancy news and
expresses a shift in her perspective on caring for beings.
Through these events, the chapter explores themes of care,
responsibility, and the emotional bonds formed with these
digital entities. Ana's journey underlines the complexities of
human attachment to artificial life, as well as the societal
perceptions around nurturing instincts. The chapter ends with
a poignant reminder of the value of all forms of
companionship, questioning the metrics by which caring is
often judged.

forums about the potential of digients to be both fun and
useful.

challenges within his personal life, including his marriage
ending. After a confrontation with his digients, Marco and
Polo, who attempted to beg for money to assert their

future as potential corporations.

companionship, ambition, and emotional bonds.
This chapter ultimately explores themes of societal evolution

entertainment, the responsibilities of digient ownership, and
the complexities of human relationships intertwined with
digital life.

In this chapter, a user group convenes to discuss a pitch from
Binary Desire, a company aiming to train digients—software

concerns by likening it to human development: just as people

digients be guided to find fulfillment.
As the group grapples with these complex ideas, Ana reflects

within the group about the nature of consent and the
implications of sexualizing digients.
Meanwhile, conversations between Derek, another group
member, and the digients Marco and Polo reveal the
complexities of autonomy and decision-making among them.

responsibilities of creators toward their creations.
