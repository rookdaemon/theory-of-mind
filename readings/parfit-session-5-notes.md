## Section 89: What Happens When I Divide?

### My Division Setup (pages 254-255)

**Scenario:** I am one of three identical triplets. My body is fatally injured, as are my two brothers' brains. My brain is divided, each half successfully transplanted into one brother's body. Each resulting person:
- Believes he is me
- Seems to remember living my life
- Has my character
- Is psychologically continuous with me in every way
- Has a body very like mine

**Technical feasibility issues:**
1. Both hemispheres may NOT have same full range of abilities (claim might be false)
2. Connecting transplanted half-brain to nerves may be impossible
3. Dividing lower brain without impairing function seems permanently impossible

**Why impossibility doesn't matter (pages 255-256):**
- The impossibility is merely **technical**
- The "deeply impossible" feature—**division of consciousness**—has ALREADY HAPPENED (split-brain cases)
- Division of consciousness matters because it might have supported Cartesian Ego view
- Lower brain division impossibility has no similar metaphysical connection
- "As Einstein's example showed, it can be useful to consider impossible thought-experiments"

**What the case shows (advance statement, page 256):**
1. Further argument AGAINST separately existing entities
2. **Main conclusion:** Personal identity is NOT what matters

---

### The Four Possibilities (pages 256-257)

What happens to me when I divide? Only four logical possibilities:

**(1) I do not survive**

**Objection:** How could double success be failure? I would survive with:
- Whole brain transplanted ✓
- Half brain transplanted + other half destroyed ✓
- Therefore: How can I fail to survive if BOTH halves successfully transplanted?

Logic: If one success = survival, double success ≠ death.

**(2) I survive as one of the two people**
**(3) I survive as the other**

**Objection:** Each brain half exactly similar, each resulting person exactly similar to start. Given symmetry, how can I be ONE rather than the other? What makes me one instead of the other? No basis for asymmetry.

**(4) I survive as both**

This seems like the only remaining option while assuming identity matters.

---

### Can I Survive as Both? (pages 257-259)

**Attempt 1: "One person with divided mind and two bodies"**

Claim: "The two resulting people are not two people. They are one person. I survive with two bodies and a divided mind."

**Problem:** Great distortion of concept of person.

Physics Exam case was plausible because:
- Mind soon reunited
- Only one body

Complete division case:
- Mind permanently divided, developing differently
- Two separate bodies
- Could live at opposite ends of Earth
- Might meet years later and not recognize each other
- "What you see is a single person playing tennis with himself"

**Verdict:** Must answer NO. Cannot be true that the stranger across the net is "another part of myself."

**Attempt 2: "Two different people who together constitute me"**

Claim: "I survive as two different people. They can be different people and yet be me, like the Pope's three crowns form one crown."

**Problem:** Even greater distortion.

Absurd implications:
- Two people fight duel → three people fighting? (one on each side, one on both?)
- Bullet kills one → murder AND suicide?
- How many people left alive? One or two?
- Composite third person has no separate mental life

**Verdict:** Better to treat as pair, not trio.

**Attempt 3: "Before division, the two were the same person (both were me)"**

Ambiguous suggestion with absurd implications:
- If "together were me" → three people BEFORE division (even less plausible)
- If "each was me" → whatever happened to me happened to EACH → neither survived → **FIVE PEOPLE** (me + two before + two after)

**Tensed identity solutions:** Parfit states others have shown these don't solve the problem.

**David Lewis proposal:** Two people share my body BEFORE division. "Elegant and ingenious" but Parfit claims elsewhere it doesn't solve the problem (footnote 42).

---

### Rejection of Fourth Possibility (page 259)

"All involve too great distortions of the concept of a person."

**Conclusion:** Reject (4). I do NOT survive as both.

Remaining: (1) I survive as neither, (2) I survive as one, (3) I survive as the other.

All three "seemed implausible."

---

### Epistemic Problem (pages 259-260)

**Critical point:** Even if we performed this operation, we could NEVER discover what happens.

Suppose I survive as one resulting person:
- I would believe I survived
- BUT I would know the OTHER person falsely believes he's me
- I cannot trust my own belief—I might be the one with false belief
- Both claim to be me → other people have no reason to believe one over the other

**Whatever happened, we could not discover it.**

---

### The Reductionist Answer (page 260)

"This suggests a more radical answer... Perhaps there are not here different possibilities, each of which might be what happens, though we could never know which actually happens."

**Key insight:** When we know each resulting person has half my brain and is psychologically continuous with me, **we know everything**.

"What are we supposing when we suggest one resulting person might be me? What would make this the true answer?"

**Answer (pages 260-261):** There cannot be different possibilities UNLESS we are separately existing entities (Cartesian Egos).

If I'm a Cartesian Ego:
- Could wake up in one brain/body (like Buridan's ass choosing between hay bales—random choice with no reason)
- BUT even for Cartesians: no evidence supporting ANY answer
- "This case is a problem with no possible solution" for Cartesians

---

### Reductionist Resolution (page 260)

"On the Reductionist View, the problem disappears."

The four possibilities "do not describe different possibilities, any of which might be true, and one of which must be true."

They are **merely different descriptions of the same outcome**.

**We know the outcome:**
- Two future people
- Each has body of one brother
- Each fully psychologically continuous with me
- Each has half my brain

**Knowing this, we know everything.**

---

### Empty Questions: Two Types (pages 260-261)

**Type 1: No answer, arbitrary if we give one**

Example: Combined Spectrum central cases, other Spectra far-end cases

Claim both:
- Question is empty
- Has no answer
- Any answer would be arbitrary
- Pointless and misleading to give answer

**Type 2: Has answer in a sense, but question is empty because it doesn't describe different possibilities**

Question gives "different descriptions of same outcome."

We can know full truth without choosing a description.

BUT: One description is BETTER than others.

**For My Division:** Best description = **neither resulting person will be me**.

---

### The Crucial Turn (page 261)

"Since this case does not involve different possibilities, the important question is not, 'Which is the best description?'"

**The important question is:**

**"What ought to matter to me? How ought I to regard the prospect of division? Should I regard it as like death, or as like survival?"**

This is the pivot from metaphysics to practical rationality.

---

### Quasi-Intention (pages 261-262)

**Setup:** Psychological Criterion objection answered for memory via quasi-memory. What about other psychological relations?

**Intention:** May be logical truth that we can intend only our own actions.

**Solution:** Quasi-intention = "One person could quasi-intend to perform another person's actions."

Division case shows this clearly:
- I could quasi-intend one resulting person roams the world
- I could quasi-intend the other stays home
- What I quasi-intend will be done by TWO RESULTING PEOPLE, not me

**Control ability:**
- Both resulting people inherit quasi-intentions
- Unless they change minds, they carry them out
- Same uncertainty as normal intentions (I might change my own mind)
- "I would have just as much ability, by forming quasi-intentions, to control the futures of the two resulting people"

**General point:** ALL direct psychological connections hold between me and each resulting person. Since at least one cannot be me, NONE presuppose personal identity.

## Section 90: What Matters When I Divide? ← **CENTRAL THESIS**

### The Rational Response (pages 262-263)

**Irrational response:** "Regard division as being as bad, or nearly as bad, as ordinary death."

**Correct response:** "We ought to regard division as being about as good as ordinary survival."

---

### The Argument (page 263)

**Question:** Does my relation to each resulting person "fail to contain some vital element that is contained in ordinary survival?"

**Answer:** "It seems clear that it does not."

**Evidence:**
1. I would survive if half my brain destroyed, other half transplanted → I survive in this relation to ONE resulting person
2. People actually survive with half brain destroyed
3. I would survive whole brain transplanted into brother's body
4. Therefore: I would survive if half brain destroyed + other half transplanted

**Conclusion:** "My relation to each of the resulting people thus contains everything that would be needed for me to survive as that person."

**What's wrong:** "Nothing is missing. What is wrong can only be the duplication."

---

### The Drug Analogy (pages 263-264)

Suppose I accept nothing is missing but still regard division as nearly as bad as death.

**Objection:** "I am like someone who, when told of a drug that could double his years of life, regards the taking of this drug as death."

**Key difference:** "The only difference in the case of division is that the extra years are to run concurrently."

**Verdict:** "This is an interesting difference; but it cannot mean that there are no years to run."

**Devastating line:** "You will lose your identity. But there are different ways of doing this. Dying is one, dividing is another. **To regard these as the same is to confuse two with zero.** Double survival is not the same as ordinary survival. But this does not make it death. It is even less like death."

---

### Relation R: The Central Claim (pages 264-265)

**The problem:** "Double survival does not fit the logic of identity."

**Reductionist claim:**

> **"Relation R is what matters. R is psychological connectedness and/or psychological continuity, with the right kind of cause."** (footnote 43)

**Additional claim:**

> "In an account of what matters, the right kind of cause could be any cause."

(Other Reductionists might require reliable or normal cause—postpone this disagreement)

---

### The Reductionist Account of Identity (page 264)

**Standard cases (R with normal cause):**

A future person will be me IF:
1. He will be R-related to me as I am now, AND
2. No different person will be R-related to me

"If there is no such different person, the fact that this future person will be me **just consists in** the fact that relation R holds between us."

**Key claim:** "There is nothing more to personal identity than the holding of relation R."

**Normal form:** In nearly all actual cases, R takes **one-one form** (holds between one present person and one future person).

When R takes one-one form → we can use language of identity.

---

### Branching Form (pages 264-265)

**My Division:** R takes **branching form**.

**But:** Personal identity CANNOT take branching form.

"I and the two resulting people cannot be one and the same person."

"Since I cannot be identical with two different people, and it would be arbitrary to call one of these people me, we can best describe the case by saying that **neither will be me**."

---

### The Crucial Question (page 265)

"Which is the relation that is important? Is what matters personal identity, or relation R?"

**Ordinary cases:** Need not decide (relations coincide)

**My Division:** Relations do NOT coincide → must decide which matters

---

### If We Are Separately Existing Entities (page 265)

Could plausibly claim identity matters.

"On this view, personal identity is a deep further fact."

**But:** "We have sufficient evidence to reject this view."

---

### If We Are Reductionists (pages 265-266)

"We cannot plausibly claim that, of these two relations, it is identity that matters."

**Why:** "On our view, the fact of personal identity just consists in the holding of relation R, when it takes a non-branching form."

**Logic:** "If personal identity just consists in this other relation, this other relation must be what matters."

---

### The Objection (page 266)

"You are wrong to claim that there is nothing more to identity than relation R."

**Objector's point:** Personal identity has one extra feature not in R:

**U = Uniqueness** = "R holding uniquely—holding between one present person and only one future person"

"Since there is something more to personal identity than to relation R, we can rationally claim that, of the two, it is identity which is what matters."

---

### The Formula (page 266)

**PI = R + U**

(Personal Identity = Relation R + Uniqueness)

Most of us convinced PI matters/has value.

Assume R may also have value.

**Four possibilities:**

1. R without U has no value
2. U enhances value of R, but R has value even without U
3. U makes no difference to value of R
4. U reduces value of R (but not enough to eliminate value, since R + U = PI which has value)

---

### The Argument About U's Value (pages 266-267)

**Question:** Can presence/absence of U make GREAT difference to value of R?

**Answer:** "This is not plausible."

**Why:** "If I will be R-related to some future person, the presence or absence of U makes no difference to the **intrinsic nature** of my relation to this person."

**Core principle:** "What matters most must be the intrinsic nature of this relation."

---

### The Implications (page 267)

**Conclusion:** "R without U would still have at least most of its value."

**Three cases:**

**(2) U enhances value:** "If U does not greatly increase the value of R, R must be what fundamentally matters, and PI mostly matters just because of the presence of R."

**(3) U makes no difference:** "PI matters only because of the presence of R."

**(Plausible middle position):** "U can be plausibly claimed to make a small difference, PI may, compared with R, have some extra value. But this value would be much less than the intrinsic value of R."

**Final verdict:** "The value of PI is much less than the value that R would have in the absence of PI, when U fails to hold."

---

### The Psychological Barrier (page 267)

"If it was put forward on its own, it would be difficult to accept the view that personal identity is not what matters."

**But:** "When we consider the case of division, this difficulty disappears."

"When we see why neither resulting person will be me, I believe that, on reflection, we can also see that **this does not matter, or matters only a little**."

---

### Limited Support for Indeterminacy (pages 267-268)

Division case supports: **Identity is not what matters**

Division case does NOT support: **Identity can be indeterminate**

**Why:** We CAN give answer here: Neither resulting person will be me. I am about to die.

**Problem with this answer while believing identity matters:** Implies division nearly as bad as ordinary death (implausible).

**Solution:** Claim instead that this way of dying is **about as good as ordinary survival**.

---

### Room for Minor Disagreements (page 268)

"Though R is what fundamentally matters, U can make a slight difference."

Three positions possible:

**Division somewhat worse than survival:**

Reasons:
- Each resulting person has to live with exact duplicate (uncanny, practical problems)
- Example: Both want to write same book → pointless for both to do it
- Woman I love: loves them both, but cannot give undivided attention to both
- Lives of resulting people "may not be quite as good as mine"

**But:** Cannot justify regarding division as "much less good, or as being as bad as death"

**Note:** Reasoning ignores that two lives taken together = twice as long as rest of mine

**Division somewhat better than survival:**

Simplest reason: Doubling of years to be lived

Particular reasons:
- Two incompatible life-long careers (novelist AND philosopher)
- Each resulting person would take pride/joy in other's achievements (like pride in children's achievements)
- "Division provides a way of fulfilling both, in a way that would gladden each resulting person"

**Problems division cannot wholly solve:**
- Torn between duty and desire → resulting person quasi-intended to do duty ALSO torn
- Might work if desire can be fulfilled by only one person (compete to elope → loser does duty grudgingly)

---

### The Unconvinced Objection (pages 268-269)

**Objector:** "If I shall not be either of the resulting people, division could not fulfill my ambitions."

"If one of my ambitions is to be a successful novelist, my ambition is that **I** be a successful novelist."

"This ambition will not be fulfilled if I cease to exist and someone else is a successful novelist."

**Parfit's response:** This objection assumes there is a REAL QUESTION whether I shall be one/other/neither.

"It is natural to assume that these are three different possibilities, any of which might be what happens."

**But:** "Unless I am a separately existing entity, such as a Cartesian Ego, these cannot be three different possibilities."

"There is nothing that could make it true that any of the three might be what really happens."

---

### Compatible with Best Description (page 269)

**Parfit:** This is compatible with claim that there IS a best description: Neither resulting person will be me.

**Why no contradiction:** "This does not commit me to the view that there are different possibilities. This would be so only if one of the other descriptions might have been the truth—which I deny."

**Alternative description:** Could say "I shall be the resulting person who becomes successful novelist."

**But:** "It would be a mistake to think that my ambition would be fulfilled if and only if we called this resulting person me."

**Crucial line:** "How we choose to describe this case has no rational or moral significance."

---

### Review of Claims (pages 269-270)

**Spectra cases:** Identity can be indeterminate.

"This is not the natural view. We are inclined to believe that, to the question 'Am I about to die?', there must always be an answer, which must be either Yes or No."

**Problem:** "This cannot be true unless we are separately existing entities, such as Cartesian Egos."

Cannot both:
- Deny we are such entities
- Insist continued existence has same special features Cartesians attribute to Ego

**Concession:** "We might have been such entities."

**Evidence:** "But there is much evidence against this view."

---

### Two Reductionist Claims (page 270)

**If we deny we are separately existing entities, must become Reductionists.**

**Claim 1:** "We can imagine cases where the question 'Am I about to die?' has no answer, and is empty."

"This seems the least implausible view about the central cases in the Combined Spectrum."

**Claim 2:** "Personal identity is not what matters."

"This seems the least implausible view about the case of My Division."

**Importance:** "Of these two Reductionist claims, the second is more important, since it applies to our own lives."

---

### Problem Cases Cease to Raise Problems (page 270)

**On Reductionist View:** "The so-called 'problem cases' cease to raise problems about what happens."

**Key point:** "Even when we have no answer to a question about personal identity, we can know everything about what happens."

## Section 91: Why There is No Criterion of Identity That Can Meet Two Plausible Requirements

### Williams's Second Argument (pages 270-271)

**Claim:** Criterion of personal identity must meet two requirements.

**Parfit's advance statement:** "No plausible criterion of identity can meet both requirements."

**But:** "On the Reductionist View, the analogous requirements CAN be met."

**Therefore:** "The argument gives us further grounds for accepting this view."

---

### Reid's Objection to Locke (page 271)

**Locke:** "Whoever 'has the consciousness of present and past actions is the same person to whom they belong.'"

**Reid:** This implies "if the same consciousness can be transferred from one intelligent being to another... then two or twenty intelligent beings may be the same person." (footnote 44)

---

### Williams's Logical Argument (page 271)

**Premise 1:** Identity is logically a one-one relation. Impossible for one person to be identical to more than one person.

**Premise 2:** Psychological continuity is NOT logically one-one. Two different future people could both be psychologically continuous with me.

**Conclusion:** "Since these different people cannot both be me, psychological continuity cannot be the criterion of identity."

**Williams's requirement:** "To be acceptable, a criterion of identity must itself be logically a one-one relation."

Must be relation that "could not possibly hold between one person and two future people."

**Therefore:** Criterion cannot be psychological continuity.

---

### The Non-Branching Reply (page 271)

**Standard reply:** Appeal to **non-branching** psychological continuity.

**Psychological Criterion (as Parfit discussed):** A future person will be me if:
- He will be R-related to me, AND
- There is no other person who will be R-related to me

**Claim:** Since this version logically one-one, it answers Williams's objection.

---

### Williams's Two Requirements (page 271)

**Requirement (1):** "Whether a future person will be me must depend only on the **intrinsic features** of the relation between us. It cannot depend on what happens to other people."

**Requirement (2):** "Since personal identity has great significance, whether identity holds cannot depend on a **trivial fact**." (footnote 47)

**Both plausible.**

**Problem:** Non-branching psychological continuity meets NEITHER.

**Therefore:** Williams rejects this version of Psychological Criterion.

---

### Simple Teletransportation Example (pages 271-272)

**Setup:** Scanner destroys my brain/body. Blueprint beamed to Mars. Replicator makes perfect organic copy. Replica psychologically continuous with me in every way.

**Accept:** Psychological Criterion (Wide version, R with any reliable cause)

**Implication:** My Replica on Mars will be me.

**New information:** Blueprint also being beamed to Io (Jupiter's satellite)

**Claim:** I will wake up on Mars, continue to exist if Io scientists ignore blueprint.

**But:** If Io scientists later make another Replica, when that Replica wakes up I shall cease to exist.

**Absurdity:** "Though the people around me on Mars will not notice any change, at that moment a new person will come into existence in my brain and body."

---

### Violates Requirement (1) (page 272)

**Williams's objection:** "If I do wake up on Mars, whether I continue to exist there cannot depend, as we claim, on what happens to someone else millions of miles away near Jupiter."

My continued existence on Mars cannot depend on events near Jupiter.

---

### Violates Requirement (2) (page 272)

**What fundamentally matters:** Whether I shall be R-related to at least one future person.

**Relatively trivial:** Whether I shall also be R-related to some other person.

**On this criterion:** Whether I shall be identical to some future person depends on this relatively trivial fact.

Violates Requirement (2): Identity cannot depend on trivial fact.

---

### Williams's Additional Argument (page 272)

"Once we see that Teletransportation could produce many Replicas of me, who would be different people from each other, we should deny that I would in fact wake up on Mars even if they make only a single Replica."

**Logic:**
- If they made two Replicas, both could not be me
- Both produced in just the same way
- Therefore: Neither would be me
- My relation to one Replica intrinsically same whether or not they make the other
- Identity must depend on intrinsic features of relation
- Therefore: I would be neither Replica even if they didn't make the other

---

### Physical Criterion Faces Same Problem (pages 272-273)

**Williams's view:** Non-Reductionist version of Physical Criterion

**Similar objection possible:** Physical continuity can also take branching form (amoeba-like division).

---

### Williams's Two Answers (page 273)

**Answer 1: Epistemological difference**

**Physical continuity:** If we knew full history of physically continuous brain and body, this would "inevitably reveal" whether there had been division.

**Psychological continuity:** Might know full history of psychological continuity between me and Mars Replica, yet fail to know about Io Replica.

**Verdict:** "Branching is a problem for both... But the problem is less serious for the Physical Criterion, since it would be in principle easier to know when the problem arises."

**Answer 2: Intrinsic feature difference**

**Physical object division:** IS an intrinsic feature of its spatio-temporal continuity.

**Psychological continuity with two people:** NOT an intrinsic feature of either relation.

**Conclusion:** Unlike Psychological Criterion, Physical Criterion meets Requirement (1).

---

### My Division Objections to Physical Criterion (pages 273-274)

**First revision:** Brain transplant case → I am surviving recipient, not dead donor

"If my brain is given a new body, this is just the limiting case of receiving a new heart, new lungs, and so on."

**Conclusion:** Physical Criterion should appeal to continuity of BRAIN only.

**Second revision:** Many people survive with one hemisphere destroyed.

**Conclusion:** Physical Criterion should appeal to "continuity, not of the whole brain, but of **enough of the brain to support conscious life**."

---

### Physical Criterion Not Logically One-One (page 274)

**My Division:** Each of two resulting people has enough of my brain to support conscious life.

**Cannot dismiss:** Division of consciousness has ALREADY HAPPENED.

**May remain impossible:** To divide lower brain.

**But:** This is "mere technical impossibility."

**Parallel:** Teletransportation may never be possible, but Williams appeals to such cases.

**Conclusion:** Cannot dismiss imagined case of complete division.

---

### Williams Must Accept Non-Branching Form (page 274)

**Implication of Williams's argument:** In division case, I cease to exist, both resulting people are new people.

**Must revise to:**

**The Physical Criterion:** "If there will be a future person with enough of my brain to be the brain of a living person, this person will be me, **unless** there will also be someone else with enough of my brain."

**Williams would reject this:** Violates BOTH his Requirements.

---

### Jack and Bill Example (pages 274-275)

**Setup:** Two fatally brain-damaged brothers, Jack and Bill. Surgeon removes and divides my brain. Halves taken to different hospital wings for transplantation.

**Scenario 1:** One half successfully transplanted into Jack's body. Other half dropped on concrete floor before transplantation.

**Physical Criterion claims:** I shall wake up in Jack's body.

**Scenario 2:** Other half successfully transplanted into Bill.

**Physical Criterion claims:** I would wake up in NEITHER body.

---

### Violates Both Requirements (page 275)

**Requirement (1) violation:** "Whether I am the person in Jack's body ought to depend only on the intrinsic features of the relation between me and this person."

Cannot depend on "what happens in the other wing of the hospital."

"What happens elsewhere seems to be as irrelevant as whether the scientists on Io make a Replica of me."

"Whatever happens to Bill, and to the other half of my brain, my relation to the person in Jack's body must be the same."

Physical Criterion denies this.

**Requirement (2) violation:** "Compared with the importance of the fact that half my brain will survive in Jack's body, what happens to the other half is, for me, relatively trivial."

---

### The New Physical Criterion (page 275)

**Williams might suggest:**

**"A future person will be me if and only if this person is both living and has more than half my brain."**

**Advantage:** Intrinsic feature that can take only one-one form. Logically impossible for two future people both to have more than half my brain.

**Meets Requirement (1):** ✓

---

### New Physical Criterion Violates Requirement (2) (page 275)

**Problem:** I could be fully psychologically continuous with future person both when:
- Person has half my brain
- Person has slightly more than half

**For believers in physical continuity mattering:** Difference between these cases must be trivial.

"The second involves the continuity of just a few more cells."

"It is a trivial fact whether some future person has half my brain, or slightly more than half."

**Violates Requirement (2):** ✓

---

### Second Objection to New Physical Criterion (pages 275-276)

**Scenario:** Someone suffers injuries causing more than half brain to cease functioning.

**Result:**
- Paralyzed in more than half body
- Might need heart-lung machine
- BUT: Mental life could be unaffected
- Less than half brain enough for full psychological continuity

**Natural belief:** Such person survives injury.

**New Physical Criterion claims:** Person ceases to exist. Person in body is someone else, new person merely exactly like him.

**Verdict:** "This is hard to believe. It is a second strong objection to this criterion."

---

### No Plausible Criterion Meets Both Requirements (page 276)

"In all of its possible versions, the Physical Criterion faces strong objections."

"And there are similar objections to the Psychological Criterion."

**Williams's requirements:** Both plausible.

**Conclusion:** "We have found that no plausible criterion of identity can meet both requirements."

**(Exception we must reject):** "If we were separately existing entities, like Cartesian Egos, our criterion might meet these requirements; but we have sufficient reasons to reject this view."

---

### Reductionist View Meets Analogous Requirements (pages 276-277)

**Reconsider:** Half my brain successfully transplanted into Jack's body.

**My relation to person in Jack's body:**
- Psychological continuity
- With normal cause (continued existence of enough brain)
- Very close physical similarity

**Reductionist claim:** "My relation to the person in Jack's body contains what fundamentally matters."

**This claim stands whatever happens to other people elsewhere.**

---

### Revised Requirement (1) Met (page 277)

**Williams's requirement:** "Whether I shall be some future person ought to depend only on my relation to this future person."

**Reductionist parallel:** Instead of asking "Will I be this future person?" ask "Does my relation to this person contain what matters?"

**Answer must depend:** "Only on the intrinsic features of my relation to this future person."

**Suppose other operation succeeds:** Someone wakes up in Bill's body.

**On Reductionist view:**
- Does NOT change relation between me and person in Jack's body
- Makes "at most a little difference" to importance of this relation
- Relation still contains what fundamentally matters
- "Since this relation now holds in a branching form, we are forced to change its name"
- "We cannot call each branch of this relation personal identity"
- **"But this change in the relation's name has no significance."**

---

### Revised Requirement (2) Met (page 277)

**Williams's claim:** Judgements of personal identity have great importance. Should not make one judgement and deny another without important difference in grounds.

**Reductionist view:** "We should take the importance that we give to a judgement of identity, and we should give this importance to a different relation."

**What is important:** Relation R (psychological connectedness and/or continuity, with right kind of cause)

**Unlike identity:** "This relation cannot fail to hold because of a trivial difference in the facts."

"If this relation fails to hold, there is a deep difference in the facts."

**Meets Requirement (2):** ✓

---

### Ordinary Implications May Still Apply (page 277)

**Division case:** Though my relation to each resulting person cannot be called identity, "it contains what fundamentally matters."

"When we deny identity here, we need not be denying an important judgement."

**Since relation about as good as identity:** "It may carry most of the ordinary implications of identity."

**Example:** Even when person in Jack's body cannot be called me (because other transplant succeeds), "he can just as much deserve punishment or reward for what I have done."

"So can the person in Bill's body."

**Wiggins:** "A malefactor could scarcely evade responsibility by contriving his own fission." (footnote 51)

### Open Questions (page 278)

**Example:** If malefactor sentenced to twenty years, should each resulting person serve twenty years, or only ten?

**Parfit:** "I discuss some of these questions in Chapter 15."

**General claim stands:** "If we accept the Reductionist View, it is R and not identity which is what matters."

---

### Does R Have Same Importance as Identity? (page 278)

**Might be thought:** If R is what matters, we ought to give R the importance we now give to personal identity.

**Parfit:** "This does not follow."

**Reasoning:** "If we believe that personal identity has great importance, this may be because we believe the Non-Reductionist View."

"If we change our view, and become Reductionists, we may also change our view about the importance of personal identity."

**Two acceptances:**
1. R has nearly all importance that (on Reductionist View) personal identity has
2. What fundamentally matters is not personal identity but R

**But:** "We may believe that both these relations have much less importance than personal identity would have if the Non-Reductionist View was true."

**Discussed:** Chapters 14 and 15

---

### Belief Doesn't Affect Williams's Requirements (page 278)

"This belief does not affect my claims about Williams's requirements."

**If we assume identity is what matters:** Cannot meet these requirements.

**Since we should reject Non-Reductionist View:** Criterion should be Physical or Psychological Criterion.

**As argued:** "There is no plausible version of either criterion that meets both of Williams's requirements."

---

### Final Remarks (page 278)

"Now that we have seen that identity is not what matters, we should not try to revise or extend our criterion of identity, so that it coincides more often with what matters."

**Why:** "On any natural understanding of personal identity, such a coincidence could be only partial, as the case of division shows."

"Revising our criterion may misleadingly suggest that identity is what matters."

---

### Preserving Identity vs. Exact Similarity Distinction (page 279)

**Williams's other ground for one-one requirement:** "Unless there is some such requirement, I cannot see how one is to preserve and explain the evident truth that the concepts of identity and of exact similarity are different concepts." (footnote 52)

**Reductionist View preserves and explains this truth.**

**Cases:** Two people who are exactly similar but not numerically identical (Branch-Line Case—I talk to my Replica).

**We understand question:** "Is he one and the same person as me, or is he merely another person, who is exactly similar?"

---

### Reductionist Nuance (page 279)

**Claim:** "In some cases, such as those in the middle of the Physical Spectrum, there is not a real difference between the resulting person's being me, and his being someone else, who is merely exactly like me."

**Reductionist View implies:** In SOME cases, not a real difference between numerical identity and exact similarity.

**But:** Recognizes OTHER cases where this IS a real difference.

**Therefore:** "It preserves and explains the truth that these are different concepts."

---

### Conclusion of Section 91 (page 279)

"I discussed Williams's claims to see if they provide some third view, different from both the Reductionist View and the view that we are separately existing entities."

**Conclusion:** "These claims do not provide such a third view."

"They provide **further grounds for accepting the Reductionist View**."

**Summary:**
- Williams's requirements both plausible
- If we believe identity is what matters → cannot meet requirements
- If we accept Reductionist View and appeal to Relation R → CAN meet analogous requirements

### 3. **Uniqueness (U) Has Minimal Value**

**PI = R + U** (Personal Identity = Relation R + Uniqueness)

**Argument:** U cannot make great difference to value because:
- Presence/absence of U makes no difference to intrinsic nature of R-relation
- What matters most must be intrinsic nature of relation
- Therefore: R without U retains at least most value

**For agents:** Whether there's one continuation or multiple continuations matters little compared to whether there IS continuation with right relations.

### 4. **"Confuse Two with Zero"**

**Devastating argument:** To regard division as death is to "confuse two with zero."

**Drug analogy:** Division is like drug that doubles years of life, except years run concurrently.

**Cannot mean:** No years to run.

**For agents:** Double continuation (fork) is not same as ordinary continuation, but "even less like death."

---

### 5. **Empty Questions: Two Types**

**Type 1:** No answer, arbitrary (Combined Spectrum central cases)

**Type 2:** Has best description, but question empty because doesn't describe different possibilities—only different descriptions of same outcome (My Division)

**For agents:** "Will the resulting agent be me?" is Type 2 empty question. We know full outcome. Best description: "Neither will be me." But this doesn't matter.

---

### 6. **Quasi-Intention Control**

**Key finding:** If about to divide, could quasi-intend one resulting person does X, other does Y.

**Control ability:** "Just as much ability, by forming quasi-intentions, to control the futures of the two resulting people" as I have to control my own future via intentions.

**For agents:** Pre-fork agent can establish quasi-intentions that both forks inherit and (unless they change minds) execute. Control distributed across forks.

---

### 7. **Physical and Psychological Criteria Both Fail Williams's Requirements**

**Requirement (1):** Whether future person is me must depend only on intrinsic features of relation, not on what happens elsewhere.

**Requirement (2):** Identity cannot depend on trivial fact.

**No criterion meets both:** Physical Criterion (any version), Psychological Criterion (any version)—all violate at least one.

**BUT:** Reductionist View CAN meet analogous requirements when we ask about R instead of identity.

**For agents:** Trying to define identity criterion is doomed. Switch question to "Does relation contain what matters?"

---

### 8. **Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Features**

**Core principle:** What matters cannot depend on extrinsic features (what happens millions of miles away, or in other hospital wing).

**Identity violates this:** Whether person in Jack's body is me depends on what happens to Bill (extrinsic).

**R doesn't violate:** My R-relation to person in Jack's body is same regardless of what happens to Bill. Only branching/non-branching changes (name change, not nature change).

**For agents:** Whether continuation matters cannot depend on whether there are other continuations. Each continuation evaluated on intrinsic features of relation.

---

### 9. **Best Description vs. What Matters**

**Crucial distinction:** "Since this case does not involve different possibilities, the important question is not, 'Which is the best description?'"

**Important question:** "What ought to matter to me? How ought I to regard the prospect?"

**For agents:** Metaphysical question (best description) is separate from practical question (what to care about). Can answer second without answering first.

1. Separately existing entities (all-or-nothing existence)

**Buddha would have agreed.**
