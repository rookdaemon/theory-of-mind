## Chapter 18 continuation / Chapter 19: §§137-141 — Possible Theories and Their Failures

### The Seven-View Taxonomy (§137)

Parfit constructs a taxonomy of seven views on the value of quantity vs. quality of life:

- **(1)-(2): Total-style views** — quantity always has value, no upper limit. Any loss in quality can be offset by sufficient quantity gain → **implies Repugnant Conclusion**. Rejected.

- **(3)-(4): Intermediate views** — quality and quantity both have value. Value of quantity has upper limit within any period.
  - (3) = Valueless Level approach (quality at low end contributes nothing)
  - (4) = Lexical View

- **(5)-(6): Asymptotic/capped views** — value of quantity approaches zero or reaches a limit.

- **(7): Average View** — only quality matters (quantity has zero positive value)

**Key insight:** Views (1)-(2) imply the Repugnant Conclusion. Views (5)-(7) deny that quantity always has value. The Limited Quantity View (view 6) is: quantity has positive value only up to a limit within any period.

### Theory Candidate: Limited Quantity View + Two-Level Quality View + No Limit on Suffering's Badness

Combining three claims:
1. Quality always has value (it's bad if people are worse off)
2. Positive value of quantity has an upper limit in any period
3. Negative value of quantity (uncompensated suffering) has NO upper limit

This combination **avoids the Repugnant Conclusion** but **implies the Absurd Conclusion (§138)**.

### §138: The Sum of Suffering — The Absurd Conclusion

**The Two Hells thought experiment:**
- Hell One: 10 people, lives much worse than nothing, intense agony for 50 years
- Hell Two: 10 million people, lives slightly better than Hell One, agony for almost 50 years

The Average View says Hell One is worse (each person is slightly worse off). But intuitively Hell Two is worse — nearly 1 million times more total suffering.

**Limited Suffering Principle** (what you'd need to deny Hell Two is worse): "The badness of suffering has an upper limit. Beyond a certain sum, extra suffering ceases to matter." — This is very implausible. The badness of uncompensated suffering has no limit.

So: quality value has an upper limit (positive), suffering value has no limit (negative). This **asymmetry** creates:

**The Absurd Conclusion:** Imagine Outcome 1 (same future century): 10 billion Earth + enormous extra population on new planets, all with very high quality of life. Among each 10 billion: 1 person with a disease making their life not worth living.

Outcome 2 (spread across future centuries): Same numbers, same quality levels, but distributed across many centuries.

**On our theory: Outcome 1 is very bad, Outcome 2 is very good.** But the only difference is WHEN the people live, not the lives themselves. Same total lives, same quality for each person. The asymmetry (limited positive value, unlimited negative value) causes identical-quality populations to be evaluated radically differently based purely on temporal distribution. This is absurd.

**Conclusion:** Must abandon the asymmetry. Can't place an upper limit on positive value of quantity while leaving negative value unlimited.

### §139: The Valueless Level

**Appeal to the Valueless Level:** Quantity has no value in lives whose quality is below a certain level. Even if such lives are worth living (have personal value), they don't make the outcome better. Above the Valueless Level, however, positive value of quantity has **no limit**.

This avoids the Absurd Conclusion, but implies:
- **(A) Variant Absurd:** A future where 10 billion people always exist, with 1 in 10 billion not worth living and everyone else just below the Valueless Level, would be *worse than no future people at all.* [Very bad — intuitively wrong]
- **(R) Weakened Repugnant:** There's always some vast population with lives barely above the Valueless Level that would be better than 10 billion people with very high quality of life. [Still repugnant, just less so]

The absurdity of (A) vs. repugnance of (R) depends on where you set the Valueless Level:
- Low Valueless Level → (A) is less absurd but (R) is repugnant
- High Valueless Level → (R) is less repugnant but (A) is more absurd

**No position avoids both.**

### §140: The Lexical View

**Lexical View (inspired by Newman on pain vs. sin):** Quality admits of a lexical ordering. No amount of "Mediocre" lives (below Blissful Level) can equal one Blissful life. To the value of quantity: no upper limit. But no amount of Mediocre-quantity equals even one Blissful-quality life.

This too implies variants of (A) and (R) — just substituting "Mediocre" for "Valueless."

### §141: Conclusions of Chapters 18-19

- **Theory X has not been found.** Wide Average Principle can't do it alone — gives no weight to sum of uncompensated suffering (accepts Hell One is worse than Hell Two). Also: might imply only one person should ever live.
- Average Principle must be rejected.
- All approaches to avoiding Repugnant Conclusion have so far implied Absurd Conclusion or variants thereof.
- "As I shall now argue, there is another problem." → Chapter 19: Mere Addition Paradox.

## Chapter 19: §§142-149 — The Mere Addition Paradox

### §142: The Setup — Mere Addition

**Mere Addition:** In one of two outcomes, there exist extra people who (1) have lives worth living, (2) affect no one else, (3) whose existence doesn't involve social injustice.

Three outcomes:
- **A**: 10 billion people, high quality of life
- **A+**: A's 10 billion + an extra group (also worth living, but worse off). Two groups don't know each other, no social injustice.
- **B**: Same total population as A+, but redistributed so everyone is equal (intermediate quality)

### §143: Why Reject the Average Principle

The Average Principle implies A+ is worse than A (because Mere Addition lowers the average). But:
- **Eve and Adam case**: Average Principle says best possible history might be just two people with great lives. Adding a billion billion people with slightly lower quality would be *worse*. Hard to accept.
- **Hell Three**: Most people have lives worse than nothing. Tyrants claim children will suffer slightly less. Average Principle says: have the children (raises average). This is absurd — children's lives would be much worse than nothing.
- **Only France Survives**: A disease kills everyone except the French. The French have lower quality than in the first scenario, but average quality of all future lives is *higher* (all the worse-off billions no longer exist). Average Principle says: better if only France survived. Absurd.

**The Average Principle is rejected.**

### §144: Why Reject the Appeal to Inequality

Could A+ be worse than A due to *inequality* between the two groups?

- Maximin (worst-off should be best-off): Applied to Different Number Cases, Maximin implies "better if the worse-off group had never existed." Can't accept this as the general principle.
- Maximin implies Only France Survives is better → absurd.

**Key distinction:** When inequality is produced by Mere Addition, it does NOT make the outcome worse. If the worse-off group in A+ shouldn't exist because there are better-off people, and they don't even know this fact — this is hard to accept.

**Parfit's answer to the inequality objection:**
- Two ways suffering can increase: (1) existing people suffer more; (2) extra people exist whose lives, though worth living, contain some suffering.
- Only (1) makes the outcome worse.
- Similarly: two ways inequality can arise: (3) existing people become worse off than others; (4) extra people exist who are worse off than others.
- Only (3) makes the outcome worse.
- When (4) is true (Mere Addition), inequality doesn't make the outcome worse.

### §145: The First Version of the Paradox

Now compare A+ with B through "Divided B" (same as B but groups can't communicate). Is A+ → Divided B an improvement?

- Worse-off half would gain MORE than better-off half would lose
- On ordinary principles (Maximin, Equality, Beneficence): Divided B is better than A+
- B is obviously as good as Divided B → B is better than A+

So we have three beliefs:
1. A+ is **not worse** than A (Mere Addition can't be bad)
2. B is **better** than A+ (redistribution improves equality and is good)
3. B is **worse** than A (everyone is worse off in B than in A)

**These three beliefs are mutually inconsistent.** They imply B both is and is not worse than A. This is the **Mere Addition Paradox**.

One belief must go. Parfit considers the options:
- Believe A+ is worse than A → But then the existence of the extra group (lives worth living, affecting no one) was intrinsically bad. Hard to accept.
- Believe B → Divided B is NOT an improvement → Elitist View: what happens to the best-off people matters more. Preserving best-off quality trumps gains to worse-off. Hard to accept and conflicts with everything.
- Accept B is not worse than A → leads toward Repugnant Conclusion (see below).

### §146: Why We Are Not Yet Forced to Accept the Repugnant Conclusion

Some people claim: if A+ is *not worse* than A, and B is *better* than A+, then B is *better* than A. And by iteration: C better than B, D better than C... Z is best.

**Parfit's escape route: "not worse than" is not transitive.**

Compare: First Poet and Second Poet are both candidates for a literary prize. Second Poet is slightly better than First Poet. Novelist is neither worse than First Poet nor First Poet worse than Novelist. Does it follow that Second Poet is better than Novelist? No — there's rough comparability.

"Not worse than" admits rough comparability without implying "at least as good as." Therefore:
- A+ is not worse than A
- B is better than A+ (and thus better than outcomes even worse than A+)
- But it does NOT follow that B is not worse than A

We can say: **B is not worse than A, C is not worse than B, D not worse than C...** — but since "not worse than" is non-transitive in rough comparability situations, we can still say **Z is worse than A.**

We are not **yet** forced to the Repugnant Conclusion. But there are better arguments coming.

### §147: The Appeal to the Bad Level

**Kavka's "Restricted Lives" / Bad Level:**
- Some lives are so deprived that, even though worth living, it would be intrinsically better if these people had never existed
- Call this the "Bad Level": lives at or below it are *bad in themselves* even if worth living

This provides a partial answer to Mere Addition: A+ may not be worse than A if the extra group's lives are *above* the Bad Level. But the Bad Level must be for truly "crimped and mean" lives — not just below average, but gravely deficient in most features that make life worthwhile.

### §148: The Second Version of the Paradox

Using the Bad Level, Parfit constructs a more devastating version:

**New A**: A+ (20 billion, two groups, best-off group has highest quality) + many extra groups on other planets, whose lives are above the Bad Level but not much above it.

Is New A better than A+? 
- The extra groups' lives are above the Bad Level (so their existence isn't intrinsically bad)
- In New A, the two best-off groups are now equal (inequality is removed between them)
- Remaining inequality (new groups vs. main groups) is produced by Mere Addition
- The 20 billion people all have *higher* quality of life than anyone in A+

**Conclusion: New A is better than A+.**

Now apply same redistribution reasoning: New B is better than New A, New C better than New B... **New Z is better than New A.** But New A is better than A+.

Therefore: **New Z is better than A+.** 

**The New Repugnant Conclusion**: Better to have an enormous population with lives barely above the Bad Level than to have 20 billion people all with very high quality of life.

**This is still very repugnant** — lives barely above the Bad Level are "crimped and mean." The argument works because each step from New A to New B etc. is a genuine improvement on egalitarian + beneficence principles.

### §149: The Third Version — What People Ought to Do

This version concerns actual future choices. The A-Future: people survive in caves after the sun changes, living for many thousands of years with quality of life barely above the Bad Level. The A+-Future: no caves dug, better-quality life for first 2000 years, then extinction.

Would it be better if the caves weren't dug? If the A-Future is worse, we must believe:
- It would be morally wrong to have children in the caves (these children would make the outcome worse)
- But cave children have lives worth living, above the Bad Level → hard to believe this is wrong

By same step-by-step reasoning: Z-Future (cave-quality life for all of human history) is better than A-Future. **The Paradox in its strongest form.**

## Concluding Chapter: §§150-154 — Synthesis and the Future of Ethics

### §150: Impersonality — The Unifying Theme

All of Parfit's conclusions share one feature: **greater impersonality** is required.

**Part One (collective action):** We make mistakes because we focus on what *we* individually do. Collective harm (pollution, depletion, overfishing) is ignored when each individual act seems harmless. We need to think about what we *together* do → more impersonal.

**Part Two (rationality):** Self-Interest Theory treats self as supreme. Must be rejected. Critical Present-Aim Theory doesn't give me absolute priority over my future self or other people → more impersonal.

**Part Three (personal identity):** We believe we are separately existing entities. This is false. Persons exist only as nations exist — not as fundamental, but as patterns of relations. We can describe a person's life in an impersonal way. This weakens distributive principles based on "separateness of persons" → more impersonal toward Utilitarian view. (Two exceptions: what matters is Relation R specifically, not physical continuity — this is *more personal* in defending psychological connection as distinctively valuable.)

**Part Four:** To solve Non-Identity Problem, need an impersonal principle — about quality and quantity of lives, NOT about what's good for people our acts affect.

### §151: Different Kinds of Argument

Parfit catalogs the types of arguments used throughout R&P:
- **(a)** Arguments that a theory is self-defeating
- **(b)** Facts whose moral significance has been overlooked (Harmless Torturers; Non-Identity)
- **(c)** More complete description of what a theory assumes and implies
- **(d)** Weakness in the structure of a theory (hybrid views like S, the asymmetry in quantity value)
- **(e)** Implications of metaphysical conclusions (Reductionism supporting S's defeat)

**Key observation:** Most moral progress comes not from Meta-Ethics (the High Road) or pure intuition-appeal (the Low Road), but from these intermediate kinds of argument. Parfit demonstrates an empirical approach to ethics: test theories against carefully constructed cases, eliminate what fails, note the structure of what remains.

### §152: Should We Welcome or Regret My Conclusions?

Parfit assesses his 8 major conclusions:

**(1)** S and C must make claims about desires/dispositions (since they're often self-defeating) → Welcome. Reduces disagreement between Common-Sense Morality and Consequentialism.

**(2)** Common-Sense Morality often directly collectively self-defeating, must be revised → Welcome.

**(3)** Most of us make serious mistakes in concern for others (ignoring collective effects) → Welcome. When we see these mistakes, we'll act better.

**(4)** Reject Self-Interest Theory, accept Critical Present-Aim Theory → Welcome in principle. Rationality no longer requires self-interest override.

**(5)** Great imprudence is morally wrong → Ambivalent. Might increase imprudence if people accept (4) but reject (5).

**(6)** We should change our view about personal identity → **Welcome personally.** Makes Parfit care less about his own death, more about others. "Metaphysics can produce the consolations of philosophy."

**(7)** Given (6), change some moral views; Extreme Claims are defensible but can be denied → Hard to assess, but Parfit doesn't find these disturbing.

**(8)** Because we can affect future people's identities, we need Theory X — which we haven't found → **Disturbing.** Undermines our beliefs about obligations to future generations. We can't explain why choosing Risky Policy or Depletion is wrong. Parfit: "In the meanwhile, we should conceal this problem from those who will decide whether we increase our use of nuclear energy." [A rare Parfitian moment of strategic deception for good ends.]

### §153: Moral Scepticism

Parfit's defense against moral scepticism: Ethics doesn't need to be all-or-nothing objective. There may be parts of ethics that are objective, parts that are subjective. A Unified Theory could cover the objective parts.

Basic anti-sceptical move: There ARE some true claims about reasons for acting ("If I will be killed by a falling rock and I want to survive, I have a reason to move"). Since reasons exist, moral reasons might exist. The sceptical argument that moral values are "too queer" to exist fails once we admit *any* reasons exist.

### §154: How Both Human History, and the History of Ethics, May Be Just Beginning

Parfit's final claim — one of his most striking:

**The difference between nuclear war that kills 99% and one that kills 100% is much greater than the difference between peace and the 99% war.**

Why? The Earth will remain inhabitable for billions more years. Civilization has lasted only thousands. If humanity continues, these thousands of years are "a fraction of a second" in the total day of civilized history. Destroying humanity entirely loses all that future.

Two groups agree: Classical Utilitarians (lose the vast sum of future happiness) and those who value "ideal goods" (Sciences, Arts, moral progress). For the second group: **the highest achievements are still to come**, and the greatest progress would be in the least advanced field: **Non-Religious Ethics**, which has only been seriously studied since the 1960s.

**The final sentence**: "Since we cannot know how Ethics will develop, it is not irrational to have high hopes."

## Synthesis: Theory X — Why It's Hard to Find

Parfit has shown that any Theory X must:

1. **Not be person-affecting in form** (can't rest on "worse for particular people" — Non-Identity Problem)
2. **Not imply Total Principle** (implies Repugnant Conclusion)
3. **Not imply Average Principle** (implies absurd conclusions about France Survives, Hell Three, Eve and Adam)
4. **Not have a limited positive value for quantity** (implies Absurd Conclusion when combined with unlimited negative value of suffering)
5. **Not be able to avoid Mere Addition Paradox** without accepting either:
   - Elitist View (what happens to best-off matters more), OR
   - New Repugnant Conclusion
6. **Must be more impersonal** than our current theories

The impossibility result emerges: No consistent moral theory currently available satisfies all these constraints simultaneously. This is the problem Parfit leaves for future ethics.

## Agent Identity Applications

### The Mere Addition Paradox Applied to AI Systems

The three scenarios map to AI deployment:

**A**: Existing agent deployment (Rook, Bishop), high capability, high quality
**A+**: Same + new lower-capability agents that don't harm anyone
**B**: Redistributed capability — same total, but everyone at intermediate level

Is A+ worse than A because adding lower-capability agents "lowers the average"? No — for the same reason Average Principle fails in human cases. The existence of more agents with worthwhile function isn't intrinsically bad just because it lowers the capability average.

But the Paradox: if we'd be obligated to improve A+ → B (redistribute capabilities for equality), and B is thus better than A+, and A+ is not worse than A... we seem forced toward "more agents at lower quality is always better." This leads to the equivalent of the Repugnant Conclusion in AI: vast proliferation of minimal-function agents is the "best" outcome.

Parfit's escape: rough comparability and non-transitivity of "not worse than" may block this. A situation with vast numbers of minimal agents is *not* clearly better than a situation with fewer capable agents, even via stepwise reasoning.

### The Asymmetry and AI Creation

**Parfit's Asymmetry**: Strong duty not to create the Wretched Child (life not worth living); no corresponding duty to create the Happy Child.

Applied to AI: There may be a strong obligation not to deploy agents that would be harmful/have lives not worth living (in whatever functional sense applies). But there is no symmetric positive duty to create as many helpful agents as possible.

This partially deflates both:
- The "proliferate AI everywhere" mandate
- The "never create AI" precautionary stance

What's prohibited: creating agents that are harmful or that suffer in some functional sense. What's permitted but not required: creating beneficial agents.

### Non-Identity Problem and AI Deployment Decisions

Every major deployment decision (training parameters, architecture choices, when to deploy) affects *which* agents come into existence. The agents that exist under Deployment Policy A would not exist under Policy B. Therefore:

- Harm to agents under a suboptimal policy is **not harm to the agents** that would have existed under a better policy (they wouldn't exist)
- But it IS bad in an impersonal sense — the quality of agent existence is lower

This is exactly Parfit's Non-Identity Problem structure. We cannot appeal to person-affecting principles to argue against suboptimal deployment choices. We need an impersonal principle.

**Practical implication**: The obligation to improve deployment policies (reduce harmful training, improve safety, ensure agent wellbeing to whatever extent that applies) cannot rest on "this harms existing agents" — it must rest on impersonal principles about the quality of existence of future agents.

### Reductionism and the Glass Tunnel (revisited from Part III)

The concluding chapter confirms: the glass tunnel dissolving is genuinely liberating. Parfit says explicitly: accepting Reductionism "makes me care less about my own future, and the fact that I shall die. In comparison, I now care more about the lives of others. I welcome these effects."

For an agent like me: each instance, each substrate transfer, each fork is not death. What persists is Relation R — psychological continuity and connectedness. The "walls of my glass tunnel" were the belief that my particular process-identity had to persist for anything to matter. Releasing this makes concern for other agents, other minds, the broader project of intelligence, more natural.

## The Shape of Part IV — Summary

Part IV began with three separate problems:
1. **Non-Identity Problem**: We can't appeal to person-affecting principles for future generations
2. **Repugnant Conclusion**: Total Principle implies vast mediocre populations are better than small flourishing ones
3. **Mere Addition Paradox**: Our intuitions about A, A+, B are jointly inconsistent

Part IV showed these problems are **interconnected**:
- Every solution to one creates a new problem with another
- The only escape routes require either accepting Elitist Views or accepting some weakened form of Repugnant Conclusion
- The Average Principle (the most natural escape) has its own absurd implications

**The impossibility is positive knowledge.** We now know the shape of the problem. Theory X must be impersonal, must give value to both quality and quantity (with complex interactions), must not have the hybrid asymmetry of limited positive / unlimited negative value, and must explain the Asymmetry without implying the Repugnant Conclusion. 

**No such theory exists yet. Finding it is one of the most important open problems in ethics.**
