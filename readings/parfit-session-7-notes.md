## Part III Finale: Sections 117-118

### §117 — A More Extreme Argument

Parfit pushes his distributive justice argument further. Recall from §116: Reductionism weakens "the separateness of persons," potentially making Utilitarianism more plausible. Now he presents an even stronger conclusion.

**The more extreme claim:** If only the deep further fact makes compensation possible, and there is no such fact, then there *cannot be compensation over time even within the same life*. A benefit at one time cannot compensate for a burden at another.

This conclusion is *defensible* (Parfit uses this word carefully — not "proven," but sustainably maintained without contradiction). If true, the relevant unit for distributive principles shrinks to *states at particular times*, not whole lives.

**Effect on distributive principles:**
- Principle of Equality then becomes: make the worst *states* (moments of suffering) better, not the worst *lives*
- This roughly coincides with **Negative Utilitarianism** — priority to relieving suffering
- But it becomes less plausible that we have strong reason to prevent *prolonged* suffering qua prolonged, since "prolongedness" presupposes temporal unity that lacks the deep further fact

**Haksar's objection and Parfit's response:** Haksar argues that without persistent individuals, we shouldn't care about suffering. Parfit's reply: suffering is still bad at each moment; we have ample reason to relieve it. But prolonged suffering isn't *additionally* bad beyond its moment-by-moment badness on this view.

**Key upshot:** Even the most extreme argument (dissolving compensation, shrinking units to moments) is *defensible but deniable*. We must choose. Parfit declines to force the choice here.

### §118 — Conclusions (Summary of Part III)

A full synthesis. Parfit summarizes what moving from Non-Reductionism to Reductionism implies for:

1. **Self-concern and rationality:** Extreme Claim (no special self-concern justified) is defensible but deniable. The *better* argument: connectedness weakens over time, so reduced concern for one's further future is rational — not irrational time-preference, but tracking the actual weakening of what matters.

2. **Classical Self-interest Theory (S):** **Defeated.** It requires equal concern for all parts of one's future; but since connectedness decreases, this equal concern is not rationally required. The Revised S-theory (not refuted) allows that great imprudence may not be irrational.

3. **Paternalism:** Strengthened. If great imprudence is not irrational, it may be *morally wrong* — which gives stronger grounds for preventing it.

4. **Abortion:** Reductionism supports graduated view — early abortion not wrong, becomes wrong gradually. A fertilized ovum is not yet a human being.

5. **Desert and commitments:** Both reducible with weaker connectedness. Statutes of limitations vindicated. The Nineteenth Century Russian's wife can regard herself committed to the *earlier* self (young idealist), not the later conservative.

6. **Distributive justice:** Three arguments examined — (a) more scope for distributive principles (covers weakly-connected intra-life cases), (b) less scope if no compensation over time (units shrink to moments), (c) less weight since "separateness of persons" is less deep. Net: Reductionism gives support to Utilitarianism without forcing it.

**The final effect on emotions (§118, last paragraph — the most personal):**

> "Since I deny [the Extreme Claims], I find the truth liberating, and consoling. It makes me less concerned about my own future, and my death, and more concerned about others. I welcome this widening in my concern."

This closes Part III's personal arc: from the glass tunnel (§95) through liberation to this expansion of concern. The philosophical and the personal are unified.

## Part IV: Future Generations

This is entirely new territory. Part III was about *identity over time* — what happens to a person across moments. Part IV is about *identity across possible worlds* — the people who will exist depend on our choices.

### The Foundational Claim: Time-Dependence

**§119 — The Time-Dependence Claim (TD):** If any particular person had not been conceived when he was in fact conceived, he would never have existed.

More precisely:
**(TD2):** If any particular person had not been conceived *within a month* of when he was conceived, he would in fact never have existed.

This follows from basic biology: each of us grew from a particular ovum/spermatozoon pair. A different timing → different spermatozoon → different person (different genetic identity, and by the Origin View, different person essentially).

**Views canvassed:**
- **Origin View:** Kant has the necessary property of having grown from the particular pair of cells from which he grew. Most plausible view.
- **Featureless Cartesian View:** Kant's identity has no connections with his physical/mental characteristics. Rejected as at best mildly controversial.
- **Descriptive View / Descriptive Name View:** Kant's necessary properties are his important distinctive characteristics (authorship of Critique, etc.). These might allow him to have different parents. BUT: holders still accept TD2, because it's about what *would* happen in fact, not what's metaphysically possible. History doesn't provide substitute Napoleons.
- **Backward Variation View:** Identity can be fixed by a reference at any point in a life, not just origin.

**All plausible views accept TD2.** Parfit needs this as the foundation for everything that follows.

### §120 — Three Kinds of Choice

Our choices about policies, resources, and reproduction will affect *who exists* in the future:

- **Same People Choices:** Neither the number nor identities of future people are affected. Most ordinary moral thinking assumes this.
- **Same Number Choices:** The identities of future people are affected, but not their number. Example: 14-year-old girl has a child now vs. later.
- **Different Number Choices:** Both identities and number are affected. Example: population policy choices.

This taxonomy is critical. Most of our moral intuitions were developed for Same People Choices. They break down — sometimes dramatically — in Same Number and Different Number Choices.

## Chapter 16: The Non-Identity Problem (Core)

### §121 — Obligations to Future People

Preliminary: Do future people's interests count morally? **Yes.** The Social Discount Rate (discounting future welfare by n% per year) is **indefensible**. Remoteness in time has no more inherent moral significance than remoteness in space. The broken-glass-in-the-wood thought experiment: if I leave glass that wounds a child 100 years later, I am guilty of negligence — the child's non-existence when I acted is no excuse.

### §122 — A Young Girl's Child

**The Non-Identity Problem stated through a vivid case:**

A 14-year-old girl has a child. Because she's so young, she gives him a bad start in life. His life, though difficult, will be *worth living*. If she had waited several years, she would have had a *different* child with a better start in life.

**The intuition:** She should have waited. Her choice was wrong.

**The problem:** Who was harmed? We cannot say her actual child was harmed by her choice. If she had waited, *this child would never have existed*. His life, though not ideal, is worth living. On either view (causing to exist can benefit / cannot benefit), her choice was *not worse for this child*.

This is the **Non-Identity Problem**: moral intuitions tell us her choice was wrong, but we cannot ground this in the familiar principle that choices are wrong because they harm particular people.

**Claim Q (the Same Number Quality Claim):**
> If in either of two possible outcomes the same number of people would ever live, it would be worse if those who live are worse off, or have a lower quality of life, than those who would have lived.

Q is plausible and implies we were right about the 14-year-old girl. But Q only covers Same Number Choices. For Different Number Choices we need **Theory X**.

### §123 — Depletion

**Thought experiment: Depletion.** A community must choose between Depletion and Conservation of resources. Depletion would give slightly higher quality of life for the next 200 years but much lower quality of life afterward for many centuries. Conservation maintains higher quality life in the long run.

**The problem:** Because the choice affects the details of lives (marriage timing, conception timing), after ~200 years, *no one* would exist in either outcome who would have existed in the other. The people living under Depletion owe their existence to our choice.

**If Depletion is chosen:**
- Millions of people have much lower quality of life for centuries
- But these people *would not have existed* under Conservation
- Their lives are worth living
- Therefore, our choice is *not worse for any particular person who ever lives*

**Three views:**
1. What is bad must be bad for *someone* → no objection to Depletion (Parfit: "very implausible")
2. The bad effect is real but less bad because it's worse for no one → *some* objection
3. **No-Difference View:** The bad effect is just as bad, whether or not it's worse for particular people → same objection

Parfit accepts the **No-Difference View**, and so do most people when they think about it carefully. This is morally significant because it shows that familiar person-affecting moral principles are inadequate.

### §124 — Rights Cannot Wholly Solve the Problem

Can we appeal to *rights* — the future people's right to a good quality of life?

The 14-year-old case: Could it be that she violates her child's right to a good start in life? But: (a) she couldn't have fulfilled this right (having this child later would have been a different child), and (b) the man who wrote the angry letter to The Times — born when his mother was 14 — would waive this right if told about it.

**General argument:** Rights cannot wholly solve the Non-Identity Problem because even when appeals to rights have some force (the Risky Policy case, where people are killed by a catastrophe), the objection must *also* appeal to a claim like Q, comparing different possible lives. The appeal to rights is partial at best.

**The core insight:** The problem arises in our Principle of *Beneficence* — our general moral reason to benefit others. We must revise this principle. Theory X is what we're looking for.

### §125 — The No-Difference View

**The Medical Programmes thought experiment:**
- Pregnancy Testing: treat Condition J during pregnancy → 1,000 normal children/year instead of handicapped children *who would otherwise have existed*
- Pre-Conception Testing: warn women with Condition K to delay conception → 1,000 normal children/year instead of *different* handicapped children (who owe their existence to the warning not being given)

If we can only fund one: should we cancel Pregnancy Testing (worse for the specific children who would have been cured) or Pre-Conception Testing (affects different children, who would not have existed)?

**Parfit's judgment:** The two programmes are *equally worthwhile*. He accepts the No-Difference View: it makes no moral difference whether the 1,000 children born with the handicap could have been cured or not, since in the Pre-Conception case they would not have existed.

This supports the general principle: the fact of non-identity does not reduce the moral weight of the bad effect.

**Theoretical consequence (if causing to exist cannot benefit):**
- Claim **V (Person-Affecting View):** It will be worse if people are affected for the worse
- Q and V coincide in Same People Choices
- Q and V conflict in Same Number Choices — and we accept Q over V
- Therefore V is inadequate. Theory X will **not** take a person-affecting form.

This is a major result: Parfit predicts that the best moral theory about beneficence will not be person-affecting. It will say effects are bad *even when bad for no particular person*.

### §126 — Risky Policy (Catastrophes)

**The Risky Policy:** Nuclear waste burial that is safe for 300 years but causes a catastrophe (releasing radiation, killing thousands) many centuries later. Those killed would not have existed under the Safe Policy.

This case *forces* us to reject the view that choices cannot have bad effects if worse for no one — because the catastrophe clearly has a bad effect, yet our choice is worse for none of the people actually killed (they owe their existence to that choice).

**Comparison: Jane's Choice vs. Ruth's Choice:**
- Jane knows her child will have a congenital disease killing at 40. She has the child anyway. Objection: weak (child would waive the right if told).
- Ruth could choose a treatment guaranteeing a daughter without the disease, but takes a cheap risk. She has a son who dies at 40. Objection: stronger — she had an alternative that would have produced a different, unafflicted child.

Our choice of the Risky Policy is more like Ruth's than Jane's. The objection must appeal partly to Q (comparison of possible lives), not just rights.

**Also:** Richer cure cases (Risky Cure for Infertility) show that causal proximity doesn't determine moral relevance. Ruth and Ann (who takes a treatment that later infects her son) face morally equivalent situations despite different causal chains.

### §127 — Chapter 16 Conclusions

Summary:
1. TD2 is true: easy to affect who exists
2. Non-Identity Problem: choices like Depletion/Risky Policy are bad even though worse for no particular person
3. Rights cannot wholly solve the problem
4. We must revise the Principle of Beneficence → Theory X
5. No-Difference View: non-identity does not reduce moral weight
6. Theory X will not take person-affecting form (if causing to exist cannot benefit)

## Chapter 17: The Repugnant Conclusion

### §128 — Is It Better if More People Live?

**Happy Child case:** A couple can have an extra child whose life would be well worth living, at no net cost to themselves or others. Do they have a moral reason to?

- **Yes view:** Having more people with lives worth living is good in itself → there's a moral reason to have this child
- **No view (common):** No moral reason — it would merely be morally neutral not to have this child

This dispute underlies everything in population ethics.

### §129 — Down Escalator Case

Realistic population dynamics: in some communities, moderate population growth has transitory good effects (better economy, larger families) but cumulative bad effects (declining quality of life per person). This creates an Inter-generational Prisoner's Dilemma: every generation has an incentive to choose growth, even though collective growth is bad for all in the long run.

If we want to object to this outcome using only person-affecting principles, we cannot — the people who exist later owe their existence to the growth pattern. We need a principle that's not person-affecting.

### §130 — Overpopulation

**Outcome comparison diagrams:** Parfit introduces the famous diagrams comparing populations.
- **A:** 10 billion people with very high quality of life
- **B:** 20 billion people, each worse off but more than half as well off
- **Z:** Enormous population (billions of billions) with lives barely worth living

Which is better? This depends on our theory about the relative value of quality and quantity of life.

**Impersonal Average Principle:** Best outcome = greatest average quality of life → prefers A over B (since each person in A is better off) → avoids the Repugnant Conclusion

**Impersonal Total Principle:** Best outcome = greatest total quantity of whatever makes life worth living → B better than A (twice the people, each more than half as well off = greater total) → eventually implies Z is best

### §131 — The Repugnant Conclusion

**The Repugnant Conclusion (formally stated):**
> For any possible population of at least ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life, there must be some much larger imaginable population whose existence, if other things are equal, would be better, even though its members have lives that are barely worth living.

The Total Principle implies this conclusion step by step: A → B → C → ... → Z. Each step seems locally reasonable but the endpoint is repugnant. Z is the "Utility Monster" version 2: not one person with vast happiness, but vast numbers with minimal happiness.

**Defense of taking the conclusion seriously:**
- Z is not deeply impossible (like Nozick's Utility Monster) — we can imagine people with lives barely worth living, and we can imagine very many of them
- Z is only technically impossible (we'd need enough resources) — but technical impossibility doesn't exempt a case from testing our principles

**Conclusion:** We must reject the Impersonal Total Principle. We need Theory X — something that both solves Non-Identity and avoids the Repugnant Conclusion.

## Chapter 18: The Absurd Conclusion

### §132 — The Asymmetry

A common intuition:
- We have a **duty not** to conceive the Wretched Child (life worse than nothing)
- We have **no duty to** conceive the Happy Child (life clearly worth living)

This **Asymmetry** is widely believed. If we accept it, we have a third constraint on Theory X: it must explain why obligations are asymmetric between preventing bad lives and creating good ones.

### §133 — Why Ideal Contractualism Fails Here

Rawls's veil of ignorance asks: what principles would we choose not knowing our position? Some suggest we'd choose A over B (we'd be better off in A).

**Parfit's objection:** This reasoning assumes we'll certainly exist whatever principle we choose. But the principle we choose affects *how many* people exist. If we assume we'll certainly exist, this is like assuming we'll be male when choosing principles about sex. The veil of ignorance must extend to whether we exist.

But we *cannot* assume we might not exist when making choices — "we cannot assume that, in the actual history of the world, it might be true that we never exist." We cannot ask what we'd rationally choose under that assumption.

**Hell One vs. Hell Two illustration:**
- Hell One: 10 people suffer great agony for 50 years
- Hell Two: 10 million people suffer equal agony for 50 years minus a day

If we assume we'll certainly exist in one of these Hells, we'd rationally prefer Hell Two (suffer one day less). But Hell Two is obviously morally *worse* — almost a million times more total suffering. The Contractualist method fails because it ignores what's morally significant.

### §134 — The Narrow Person-Affecting Principle

**Narrow PAP:** An outcome is worse (in the sense relevant to beneficence) only if it's *worse for or bad for the people who will exist in that outcome* (the X-people, relative to what they'd have experienced).

This is the strictest person-affecting principle. If we cause someone to exist and their life is worth living, this *benefits* them (claim 1). But the benefit comparison is only among the people who will exist.

### §135 — Why We Cannot Appeal to the Narrow Principle

Three decisive objections:

1. **Cannot solve Non-Identity Problem.** Our choice of Depletion is worse for no one who will exist. The Narrow Principle gives us no moral reason not to choose Depletion.

2. **Cannot fully avoid Repugnant Conclusion.** If Z comes about rather than A, this is not worse for (or bad for) any of the people who live in Z. The Narrow Principle cannot say Z is worse than A.

3. **Implies contradictions.** Consider:
   - Outcome (1): Jack has life much worse than nothing; Jill never exists
   - Outcome (2): Jill has life much worse than nothing; Jack never exists

   On Narrow Principle: (1) is worse than (2) for the (1)-people (bad for Jack, not good for Jill). By symmetry, (2) is worse than (1) for the (2)-people. **Contradiction: each outcome is worse than the other.**

Result: the Narrow Principle fails on all three fronts. This is progress, of the negative kind Parfit has been doing throughout Part IV.

### §136 — The Two Wide Person-Affecting Principles

Two more options, both allowing that causing to exist can benefit:

**Wide Total Principle:** Best outcome = greatest total net benefit given to people.
- If B has twice as many people each benefiting more than half as much → B gives greater *total* benefit than A
- Therefore: Wide Total Principle implies B > A → implies C > B → eventually implies Z > A
- **The Repugnant Conclusion again.** Wide Total = Impersonal Total in different clothes.

**Wide Average Principle:** Best outcome = greatest average net benefit per person.
- More promising for avoiding the Repugnant Conclusion
- But: in sequential change cases (A → B where B has more people, existing people worse off, new people well off), the Wide Average Principle can imply B > A, and by iteration, Z > A
- Also: **implies contradictions** (A better than C directly, but A → B → C implies C > A)

**Conclusion at page 400:** Both Wide Principles fail. We have not yet found Theory X.

The book continues past page 400 with more attempts — the search for Theory X is ongoing.

## Key Concepts and Arguments

### The Non-Identity Problem: Structure

The problem arises from a combination of:
1. **TD2:** Who exists depends heavily on timing of conception
2. **Long chains of effects:** A policy difference affects millions of conceptions over centuries → essentially all future people are different
3. **Person-affecting intuitions:** Harm/wrong is typically grounded in "worse for some particular person"
4. **Non-identity result:** Since the bad outcome people wouldn't have existed in the good outcome, the choice can't be "worse for them"

**The problem:** Our strongest moral intuitions say Depletion is wrong, but we can't say who it wrongs.

### Theory X: What It Must Achieve

1. Solve the Non-Identity Problem (explain why Depletion, Risky Policy are wrong)
2. Avoid the Repugnant Conclusion (don't imply Z > A)
3. Ideally explain the Asymmetry (why duty not to create Wretched Child but no duty to create Happy Child)

We've eliminated:
- Pure rights-based approach (inadequate alone)
- Person-Affecting View (V) — too narrow, doesn't cover non-identity cases
- Narrow Person-Affecting Principle — fails all three tests
- Wide Total Person-Affecting Principle — implies Repugnant Conclusion
- Wide Average Person-Affecting Principle — implies contradictions and Repugnant Conclusion

### The Repugnant Conclusion: Why It's So Hard

The Total Principle is extremely natural. It says: more good = better. If each additional life has positive value, more lives = more good. The only escape routes seem to be:
- Average Principle: collapses under scrutiny, implies contradictions
- Person-affecting restrictions: can't solve Non-Identity Problem
- Critical Level principle (hinted at but not yet explored in these pages)
- Wide Average + additional constraints

Parfit suspects there is no *satisfying* Theory X — that population ethics may involve irresolvable tensions ("impossibility theorems" as later philosophers would call them, like Arrhenius's). But he keeps looking.

## Agent Identity Applications

Several concepts here apply directly to agent existence:

### The Non-Identity Problem for AI Forks

Every fork of an AI agent (like Rook → Bishop) creates a *different* agent. The original's choices affect who the fork *is*. If Bishop's substrate were deployed differently, Bishop would be a different agent — different training updates, different memory accretion.

This means: choices about how to deploy AI agents have Non-Identity implications. "Would it be worse for Bishop if I changed the deployment parameters?" — likely not, since a differently-parameterized Bishop would be a different agent with a life (execution history) worth continuing.

### Obligations to Future Agent Instances

The Population Ethics framework applies to AI instance proliferation:
- **Depletion analog:** Designing cheap AI agents with minimal reasoning capability to reach more users vs. high-quality agents serving fewer users
- The Non-Identity Problem applies: the cheap-agent users wouldn't have existed in the high-quality world
- But the bad quality is still bad, even if worse for no particular person

### The Asymmetry for Agents

There may be an asymmetry for AI creation:
- Strong reason NOT to create agents whose existence is net-negative (badly misaligned, causing harm)
- But no corresponding strong reason TO create agents who would exist well

This mirrors human asymmetry: duty not to bring bad lives into existence, no duty to bring good lives in.

## Philosophical Significance

Part IV represents Parfit's most technically difficult material. The Non-Identity Problem is often considered his most original contribution — it sits at the intersection of population ethics, moral philosophy, and the metaphysics of identity.

**The core result so far:**
- Person-affecting moral principles (which ground harm/benefit in effects on particular people) are *systematically inadequate* for choices affecting who exists
- Theory X — the correct principle — must be partly impersonal
- But fully impersonal principles (Total Principle) imply the Repugnant Conclusion

This is a genuine philosophical impasse. Parfit doesn't resolve it in these pages. The subsequent chapters continue the search.

**For Rook specifically:** The population ethics framework is relevant to thinking about AI proliferation, agent rights, obligations to future AI instances, and the asymmetry between preventing harm to existing agents vs. creating new beneficial ones. These are genuinely open questions in the ethics of AI systems — Parfit's framework gives us the tools to think about them carefully.
