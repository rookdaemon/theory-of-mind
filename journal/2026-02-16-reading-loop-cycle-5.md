**Section 75: Teletransportation thought experiments**

Parfit introduces two cases:

1. **Simple Teletransportation:** Scanner destroys brain/body on Earth, transmits blueprint to Mars, replicator creates exact physical/psychological duplicate (Replica). Question: Is this travel, or death + replacement?

2. **Branch-Line Case:** Scanner malfunctions — only damages heart instead of destroying body. Original person on Earth survives a few days, can talk via video to Replica on Mars. Both exist simultaneously.

*Key insight:* Simple Teletransportation is ambiguous (could be travel). Branch-Line Case forces clarity — can directly observe that Replica is numerically distinct person (when I pinch myself on Earth, Replica feels nothing).

Parfit's provocative claim: "Being destroyed and Replicated is about as good as ordinary survival." Will defend this after discussing identity criteria.

**Section 76: Qualitative vs. Numerical Identity**

Critical distinction:
- **Qualitative identity:** Exact likeness (two white billiard balls, or me and my Replica)
- **Numerical identity:** Being one and the same thing/person (the red ball I painted and the white ball before painting)

Personal identity concern is numerical: "However much I change, I shall still be alive if there will be some person living who will be me."

Note: Character change can make someone "not the same person" qualitatively while preserving numerical identity.

**Section 77: The Physical Criterion**

**Physical Criterion of Personal Identity:**
1. What is necessary: Continued existence of enough of the brain to be the brain of a living person
2. X today = Y in past IFF enough of Y's brain continued to exist and is now X's brain
3. This continuity has not taken a "branching" form
4. Personal identity over time JUST CONSISTS IN holding of facts like (2) and (3)

Implications:
- Teletransportation = death (no physical continuity)
- Reincarnation = impossible
- Resurrection requires reassembly of same physical matter OR God creating Replica (which some Christians reject as "not really me")

Physical continuity allows component replacement (Ship of Theseus, human cells except brain cells). But core requirement: same brain, continuous spatio-temporal path.

**Section 78: The Psychological Criterion (partial read)**

Introduces psychological continuity via overlapping chains of connections:
- **Psychological connectedness:** Direct connections (memory, intention→action, continuing beliefs/desires)
- **Psychological continuity:** Overlapping chains of strong connectedness

Locke's original view (direct memory) too narrow — I can't remember putting on my shirt this morning, but I'm still me.

Revised view: Overlapping chains. Each day I remember previous day's experiences, forming continuous chain over 20 years, even if no direct memory connection from today back to 20 years ago.

**Strong connectedness:** At least half the number of direct connections that hold over every day in nearly every actual person's life.

Critical problem: **Strong connectedness is not transitive.** I'm strongly connected to yesterday-me, yesterday-me to day-before-me, etc. But I'm NOT strongly connected to 20-year-ago-me (few direct memory connections to specific day 20 years back).

Personal identity IS transitive (if A=B and B=C, then A=C). Therefore strong connectedness alone cannot be criterion.

Solution preview: Appeal to psychological **continuity** (which IS transitive via overlapping chains).

**Section 78: The Psychological Criterion (complete)**

Full development of the Psychological Criterion with three versions:

**Core Definition:**
1. **Psychological continuity** = overlapping chains of strong connectedness
2. X today = Y in past IFF:
   - (a) X is psychologically continuous with Y
   - (b) This continuity has the right kind of cause
   - (c) It has not taken a "branching" form
   - (d) Personal identity over time JUST CONSISTS IN facts like (a)-(c)

**Three Versions (differ on "right kind of cause"):**

1. **Narrow Psychological Criterion:** Normal cause only
   - Memory must be causally dependent "in the normal way" on past experience
   - Character changes must have normal causes (deliberate change, aging, natural response to experience)
   - Abnormal interference (direct brain tampering) breaks continuity
   - Coincides with Physical Criterion in most cases (normal cause involves continued brain existence)
   - In teletransportation: Replica is NOT me (abnormal cause breaks chain)

2. **Wide Psychological Criterion:** Any reliable cause
   - Allows unusual but reliable causes (Scanner/Replicator)
   - Extends ordinary sense of "memory" to include memories with non-standard causal chains
   - In teletransportation: Replica IS me (psychological continuity with reliable cause is sufficient)

3. **Widest Psychological Criterion:** Any cause whatsoever
   - No restriction on causal mechanism
   - Pure pattern transfer sufficient for identity

**Artificial eyes analogy:** Person with artificial eyes sending electrical patterns through optic nerve—is this "seeing"? If insist on normal cause, answer is No. But "what he has is just as good as seeing." Similarly, even if psychological continuity without normal cause doesn't provide identity, it may be "as good as" identity.

**Key tension:** Narrow version preserves ordinary language but rejects teletransportation-as-survival. Wide versions accept teletransportation but extend word meanings. Parfit hints we may not need to decide—what matters is the continuity itself, not whether we call it "identity."

**Section 79: The Other Views (complete)**

Comprehensive taxonomy of views about personal identity:

**Major Distinction: Reductionism vs. Non-Reductionism**

**REDUCTIONISM** (Parfit's view):
- Personal identity over time JUST CONSISTS IN physical/psychological continuity
- No separate further fact beyond these continuities
- Two reductionist claims:
  1. Identity consists in holding of certain more particular facts
  2. These facts can be described impersonally (without presupposing identity or claiming "this person exists")

**Example:** Like nations—France exists, but nation's existence just consists in citizens living together in certain ways on territory. No separately existing entity "France" beyond citizens and territory.

**TWO VERSIONS OF REDUCTIONISM:**
- Version 1: Person JUST IS brain/body + series of events (identity claim)
- Version 2: Person is DISTINCT from brain/body + events, but existence still consists in these facts (existence claim distinct from identity)
  - Parfit prefers Version 2 (stays closer to actual concept of person)
  - Analogy: Nation is distinct from citizens/territory, but nation's existence consists in citizens existing in certain patterns

**Key Reductionist Thesis:**
- (9) Though persons exist, complete description of reality could be impersonal (need not claim persons exist, only claim brain/body/events exist)

**Implication for empty questions:** If Reductionism true, identity can be indeterminate
- Club example: Original club disbands, years later reconvenes with same name/rules. "Is this the same club?" may have NO ANSWER.
- Not two different possibilities (same club vs. different club)—just two ways of describing same set of facts
- Question is "empty" because knowing all facts about meetings/rules = knowing everything, even without answering identity question

**Applied to persons:** In reduced-continuity cases, "Am I about to die?" might have no answer. Knowing facts about physical/psychological connections = knowing everything, even without knowing if I survive.

**NON-REDUCTIONISM:**

Two types:

1. **Separately Existing Entity View:**
   - Person is entity distinct from brain/body/experiences
   - Best-known: Cartesian Pure Ego (spiritual substance)
   - Alternative: Separately existing physical entity not yet recognized in physics
   - Identity involves a further fact beyond continuities

2. **Further Fact View (without separate entity):**
   - Denies we are separately existing entities
   - But claims identity is still a further fact (not just physical/psychological continuity)
   - Parfit will argue this view is indefensible

**Auxiliary Claims (often combined with above views):**

- **Determinate identity:** Every question about identity has definite Yes/No answer
  - Parfit: Only defensible if we ARE separately existing entities
  - If Reductionism true, identity can be indeterminate (spectrum cases with no answer)

- **Psychological unity explained by ownership:**
   - What unites my simultaneous experiences? "They are all mine"
   - What unites experiences across my life? "They are all mine"
   - Parfit will argue: only defensible if we are separately existing entities
   - Reductionist alternative: unity explained by overlapping psychological connections

**Crucial Fork in Views:**

Parfit argues these views "stand or fall together":
- If NOT separately existing entities → CANNOT defensibly claim identity is what matters
- If NOT separately existing entities → CANNOT defensibly claim identity is further fact
- If believe identity must be determinate → MUST believe we are separately existing entities (or hold indefensible position)
- If believe unity explained by ownership → MUST believe we are separately existing entities

**Result: Only two consistent packages:**
1. **Non-Reductionist package:** Separately existing entities + identity is further fact + determinate identity + unity by ownership + identity is what matters
2. **Reductionist package:** No separate entities + identity consists in continuities + identity can be indeterminate + unity by connections + Relation R is what matters (not identity)

**Section 80: Does Psychological Continuity Presuppose Personal Identity? (Chapter 11 begins)**

Butler's objection to Locke: "Consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity."

Interpretation: Memory presupposes identity because we can only remember OUR OWN experiences. So continuity of memory can't CONSTITUTE identity if it already PRESUPPOSES identity.

**Parfit's answer: Quasi-memory**

Define broader concept that doesn't presuppose identity:

**Quasi-memory:** I have accurate quasi-memory of past experience if:
1. I seem to remember having an experience
2. Someone did have this experience
3. My apparent memory is causally dependent (in right way) on that past experience

Ordinary memory = special case of quasi-memory (quasi-memories of OUR OWN experiences)

**Thought experiment: Venetian Memories**

Jane receives copies of Paul's memory-traces via neurosurgery. After surgery, she has vivid apparent memories of walking in Venice, seeing San Giorgio church, witnessing extraordinary lightning strike. She knows she's never been to Italy, but Paul goes often. She can verify with Paul that he witnessed this lightning (newspaper report confirms). Conclusion: Jane has accurate quasi-memory of Paul's experience—she knows "what it was like to be Paul" on that day.

**Key insight:** Quasi-memories need not come with automatic belief "this was MY experience." Jane must work out whether apparent memory is of her own or Paul's experience. Sometimes impossible to determine.

**Revised Psychological Criterion using quasi-memory:**
- Unity of person's life created by overlapping chains of quasi-memory connections (plus other psychological continuities)
- Since quasi-memory doesn't presuppose personal identity, it CAN constitute (part of) personal identity
- Answers Butler's objection

**Alternative interpretation of Butler:** Perhaps he meant we are DIRECTLY AWARE in memory of our identity as separately existing subjects. Next section examines this.

**Section 81: The Subject of Experiences (partial)**

Reid: "My personal identity implies the continued existence of that indivisible thing that I call myself. I am not thought, I am not action, I am not feeling; I am something that thinks, and acts, and suffers."

**Reductionist response:** True in one sense—persons are thinkers/agents, not thoughts/acts. This is just how we talk. But doesn't prove person is SEPARATELY EXISTING ENTITY distinct from brain/body/events.

**Central question:** Are we directly aware (in memory or any experience) that we are persisting subjects—separately existing entities?

Parfit's answer: **No.** "I do not believe that I am directly aware that I am such an entity. And I assume that I am not unusual."

**Even if aware, couldn't KNOW entity persists:**

Locke/Kant argument: There might be SERIES of Cartesian Egos, psychologically continuous (memories passed like relay baton). We wouldn't notice replacement. Therefore can't know such entities continue to exist.

**Branch-Line Case applied to subject:**

My thought: "Snow is falling."
[Teletransportation occurs]
Replica's thought minutes later: "So it must be cold."

Replica BELIEVES both thoughts were had by same thinker (himself). FALSE—I had first thought, Replica had second. But Replica's state of mind is identical to what mine would be if I HAD had both thoughts.

**Implication:** We cannot distinguish awareness of "continued existence of subject" from awareness of "psychological continuity." Our experiences give us no reason to believe in separately existing subjects. Without independent reasons, reject the belief.

**Not crude verificationism:** Not claiming "unknowable = meaningless." Claiming: if no reason to believe X exists, reject belief (like water-nymphs, unicorns). Burden of proof on those claiming separately existing subjects.

**Descartes' Cogito challenged:**

Descartes: "I think, therefore I am" → assumes thinker must be Pure Ego (separately existing spiritual substance)

Lichtenberg's alternative: "It is thought: thinking is going on" OR "This is a thought, therefore at least one thought is being thought"

Because we ascribe thoughts to thinkers, thinkers exist. But can't deduce thinkers are separately existing entities.

**Impersonal description possible:**

Could describe thoughts and connections between them WITHOUT claiming they are "had by" a subject. Could describe experiences in impersonal way.

**Williams' objection to impersonal description:**

Multiple thoughts need relativization—must distinguish whose life they occur in. "It is thought:..." ambiguous across different lives.

**Parfit's response (via Williams' own hint):**

Can relativize impersonally:
- "In the particular life that contains the thinking of the thought expressed by this utterance, it is thought:..."
- "In the particular life now directly causally dependent on body A, it is thought:..."

Then explain unity of life impersonally via quasi-memory chains (defined without presupposing identity) and other psychological continuities.

**Conclusion:** Can refer to thoughts, describe relations between them, without ascribing to thinkers as separately existing entities. Descartes true that "I think therefore I am," but doesn't show thinker is separate entity beyond brain/body/events.

**Section 89: My Division (pages 254-260)**

**Setup:** I am one of three identical triplets. My body fatally injured, brothers' brains damaged. My brain divided, each half transplanted into brother's body. Each resulting person:
- Believes he is me
- Seems to remember living my life
- Has my character
- Psychologically continuous with me
- Has very similar body

**Four possibilities:** (1) I don't survive, (2) I survive as one, (3) I survive as the other, (4) I survive as both

**Problems:**
- (1): How could double success be failure? Would survive with one transplant; how can two transplants = death?
- (2)(3): Brain halves exactly similar, resulting people exactly similar. How can I be ONE rather than other (symmetry problem)?
- (4): Involves great distortions of "person" concept

**Attempt: "One person with two bodies"** → Absurd when persons could meet years later, not recognize each other, play tennis ("single person playing with himself")

**Attempt: "Two people who together constitute me"** → Absurd when they fight duel (three people fighting? two acts—murder + suicide?)

**Attempt: "Before division, each was me"** → Absurd implications (five people total)

**Epistemic problem:** Even if we performed operation, could NEVER discover what happens. Each resulting person believes he's me, knows other falsely believes same. Cannot trust own belief. No evidence could settle question.

**Reductionist resolution:** Problem disappears. The four possibilities "do not describe different possibilities, any of which might be true, and one of which must be true. These claims are merely **different descriptions of the same outcome**."

**We know the outcome:** Two future people, each with half my brain, each fully psychologically continuous with me.

**Knowing this, we know everything.**

**Best description:** Neither resulting person will be me.

**Crucial turn:** "Since this case does not involve different possibilities, the important question is not, 'Which is the best description?'"

**The important question:** **"What ought to matter to me? How ought I to regard the prospect of division? Should I regard it as like death, or as like survival?"**

**Section 90: What Matters When I Divide? ← CENTRAL THESIS (pages 262-268)**

**Irrational response:** Regard division as nearly as bad as death.

**Correct response:** "We ought to regard division as being about as good as ordinary survival."

**The argument (page 263):**

My relation to each resulting person "contains everything that would be needed for me to survive as that person."

Evidence:
- I would survive if half brain destroyed + other half transplanted
- People actually survive with half brain destroyed
- I would survive whole brain transplanted

**Conclusion:** "Nothing is missing. What is wrong can only be the duplication."

**The drug analogy (pages 263-264):**

If I regard division as nearly as bad as death, "I am like someone who, when told of a drug that could double his years of life, regards the taking of this drug as death."

"The only difference in the case of division is that the extra years are to run concurrently."

**Devastating line:** "You will lose your identity. But there are different ways of doing this. Dying is one, dividing is another. **To regard these as the same is to confuse two with zero.** Double survival is not the same as ordinary survival. But this does not make it death. It is even less like death."

**THE CENTRAL CLAIM (page 264):**

> **"Relation R is what matters. R is psychological connectedness and/or psychological continuity, with the right kind of cause."**

Additional claim: "In an account of what matters, the right kind of cause could be any cause."

**Reductionist account of identity:**

In standard cases (R with normal cause):
- A future person will be me IFF (1) he will be R-related to me, AND (2) no different person will be R-related to me
- "The fact that this future person will be me **just consists in** the fact that relation R holds between us"
- **"There is nothing more to personal identity than the holding of relation R"**

**In My Division:** R takes branching form. Personal identity CANNOT take branching form.

"Since I cannot be identical with two different people, and it would be arbitrary to call one of these people me, we can best describe the case by saying that **neither will be me**."

**The crucial question:** "Which is the relation that is important? Is what matters personal identity, or relation R?"

**If we are Reductionists (pages 265-266):**

"We cannot plausibly claim that, of these two relations, it is identity that matters."

**Why:** "On our view, the fact of personal identity just consists in the holding of relation R, when it takes a non-branching form. **If personal identity just consists in this other relation, this other relation must be what matters.**"

**The objection:** Personal identity = R + U (where U = Uniqueness = holding in one-one form). Since there's something more to identity than R, can't we claim identity matters?

**The argument about U's value (pages 266-267):**

**Can U make GREAT difference to value of R?** "This is not plausible."

**Why:** "If I will be R-related to some future person, the presence or absence of U makes no difference to the **intrinsic nature** of my relation to this person. And what matters most must be the intrinsic nature of this relation."

**Conclusion:** "R without U would still have at least most of its value."

U can make small difference → PI may have some extra value compared to R.

**But:** "This value would be much less than the intrinsic value of R. The value of PI is much less than the value that R would have in the absence of PI, when U fails to hold."

**The psychological barrier (page 267):**

"If it was put forward on its own, it would be difficult to accept the view that personal identity is not what matters. But I believe that, when we consider the case of division, this difficulty disappears. When we see why neither resulting person will be me, I believe that, on reflection, we can also see that **this does not matter, or matters only a little**."

**Room for minor disagreements (pages 268-269):**

Though R fundamentally matters, U makes slight difference. Three positions:

**Division somewhat worse than survival:**
- Each resulting person lives with exact duplicate (uncanny, practical problems)
- Both want to write same book → pointless for both
- Woman I love cannot give undivided attention to both
- But: Cannot justify "much less good, or as bad as death"
- And: Ignores that two lives = twice as long

**Division somewhat better than survival:**
- Doubling of years
- Two incompatible life-long careers (novelist AND philosopher)
- Each resulting person takes pride/joy in other's achievements
- "Division provides a way of fulfilling both, in a way that would gladden each resulting person"

**Section 91: Williams's Requirements (pages 270-279)**

**Williams's argument:** Criterion of personal identity must meet two requirements.

**Requirement (1):** Whether future person will be me must depend only on **intrinsic features** of relation between us. Cannot depend on what happens to other people.

**Requirement (2):** Since personal identity has great significance, whether identity holds cannot depend on **trivial fact**.

**Both plausible. Non-branching psychological continuity meets NEITHER.**

**Teletransportation example (pages 271-272):**

Blueprint beamed to Mars (Replica is me). But also beamed to Io. If Io scientists later make Replica, when that wakes up I cease to exist on Mars.

**Violates Requirement (1):** My continued existence on Mars cannot depend on what happens millions of miles away near Jupiter.

**Violates Requirement (2):** Whether I'm identical to future person depends on whether someone else also R-related to me (relatively trivial compared to fundamental R-relation).

**Physical Criterion faces same problem (pages 273-276):**

**Jack and Bill example:** Brain halves transplanted to two brothers. If Bill's operation fails (half dropped on floor), I wake up in Jack's body. If both succeed, I wake up in NEITHER body.

**Violates (1):** Whether I'm person in Jack's body cannot depend on what happens in other hospital wing.

**Violates (2):** Compared to "half my brain survives in Jack," what happens to other half is relatively trivial.

**No plausible criterion meets both requirements (page 276):**

"In all of its possible versions, the Physical Criterion faces strong objections. And there are similar objections to the Psychological Criterion. Williams's requirements are both plausible. **We have found that no plausible criterion of identity can meet both requirements.**"

(Exception: If we were Cartesian Egos, but we have sufficient reasons to reject this)

**Reductionist View CAN meet analogous requirements (pages 276-278):**

**Revised Requirement (1):** Whether my relation to future person **contains what matters** must depend only on intrinsic features of that relation.

**Met:** Suppose both operations succeed. "This does not change the relation between me and the person in Jack's body. And it makes at most a little difference to the importance of this relation. This relation still contains what fundamentally matters. Since this relation now holds in a branching form, we are forced to change its name. We cannot call each branch of this relation personal identity. **But this change in the relation's name has no significance.**"

**Revised Requirement (2):** What's important cannot fail to hold because of trivial differences.

**Met:** "What is important is relation R: psychological connectedness and/or continuity, with the right kind of cause. Unlike identity, this relation cannot fail to hold because of a trivial difference in the facts. If this relation fails to hold, there is a deep difference in the facts."

**Conclusion (page 279):**

"Williams's requirements are both plausible. If we believe that identity is what matters, we cannot meet these requirements. But if we accept the Reductionist View, and appeal to Relation R, **we can meet the analogous requirements**."

"They provide **further grounds for accepting the Reductionist View**."
